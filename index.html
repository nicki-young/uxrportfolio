Case study · Ocean data platform

OCN

OCN uses mapping, buoy-mounted sensors, and AI to track and forecast ocean health. I helped define who the MVP was for, shaped the site's information architecture, and owned the map.

Role
UX Researcher
Timeline
Dec 2023 – Aug 2024 (Phase III)
How I joined
Applied directly to OCN's VP of Strategy when the team was looking for researchers
Team
2 UX researchers, 2 UX designers, CEO and VP of Strategy
Tools
Moqups, Figma
Methods
Market research · HMW analysis · Journey maps · Task flows · Spatial aggregation research · Storyboarding workshops · Lo- and mid-fi wireframing · Usability testing

Context

OCN aims to unite the world's ocean data and make collecting it practical. When I joined, it had no way to display that data. Our team of four was brought on to launch the MVP, with three goals:

  1. Find user pain points and business opportunities in the ocean-mapping market.
  2. Build a product demo for investor meetings.
  3. Work with Design on an accessible, AI-assisted mapping dashboard, and test it with users.

Strategy and flows

Discovery was mostly done, but the team was stuck on how to show data on the map, chart ocean metrics, and organize pages. While my colleague built Jobs-to-be-Done personas, I led the mapping research. Drawing on my GIS expertise and other platforms, I answered all 21 How-Might-We statements from earlier phases and narrowed the work to three problems to solve before launch:

  • Scaling complexity up or down for different users
  • Layering multiple data sets cohesively
  • Comparing elements over time

I mapped task flows for every Required MVP feature (such as saved searches, switching from Map View to Data Analysis, and viewing data over time) and a journey map for each persona, tracing their path through the dashboard to the information they need, like monitoring areas of concern or finding healthy areas to invest in.

Mapping research

The team kept stalling on what spatial unit to aggregate ocean data to. I found there was no universal standard, identified four models OCN could follow, and presented them in a research report.

Recommendation

Match the standard of the satellite imagery OCN is likely to use. It integrates seamlessly and scales up to remote areas that need less detail.

Investor demo

Mid-sprint, the CEO asked for a demo for upcoming investor meetings. We storyboarded, sketched, and voted on screens; I then led wireframing in Moqups, iterating twice before handing off to the designers.

2 weeksto finish the demo, instead of the planned 4

I also worked on the map display and a chart with one designer, and designed the map icons from the OCN logo. We treated the CEO's first meeting as a usability test, then improved navigation back to the dashboard, the AI chatbot flow, and several charts. Remaining gaps: Figma's small touch targets during live demos, objects left unclickable to save time, and validation with more users, which we addressed in the next phase.

Developing the MVP

I took the Zone Details page from lo-fi toward hi-fi. It covers a location's zones, zone health, conditions like salinity, turbidity, acidity, and temperature, and trends. Each version responded to feedback from the designers and a VP, and some cut components came back later. Some information is redacted for privacy.

Once the designs reached hi-fi, we ran limited user testing on the dashboard. It caught bugs before launch and gave us clear direction for the next iteration.

Takeaways

  • I shaped the dashboard’s strategy, not just its screens. Resolving the 21 open questions, defining the three problems to solve before launch, and recommending the spatial data standard gave the team a clear direction for the MVP.
  • I worked as strategist, researcher, and lo-fi designer, and layering those roles made the product stronger.
  • I became the team's expert on mapping and the science, learning what I didn't know by knowing where to look.
  • Our user groups shifted as scope changed, and we couldn't test every iteration, so we used live products for early validation and saved user testing for the hi-fi designs.

Case study · Community food sharing

Food Distro

A mobile app for sharing surplus food with neighbors. Our research settled who it was for, which business model to follow, and how it should be organized.

Role
UX Researcher
Timeline
Feb – Apr 2023 (Phase I)
How I joined
Client project through Tech Fleet
Deliverables
Affinity maps, data visuals, personas, IA site map, hi-fi clickable prototype (MVP)
Tools
Userbit, Optimal Workshop
Methods
Competitive analysis · SWOT · Survey (174 started, 138 completed) · 7 semi-structured interviews · Inductive coding · Affinity mapping · Personas · Card sort

Context

Through Tech Fleet, a community that pairs people breaking into tech with real projects, client Katia S. brought an idea: connect people with neighbors and local stores that have surplus food.

"Why not share it with someone who is in need — like a new mother who doesn't have time to make any food or your elderly neighbor who struggles to make ends meet?"

The challenge was making sharing easy for givers while giving receivers confidence in the food's quality and hygiene. We set out to identify target users and their motivations and barriers, size up competitors, and find patterns to guide the design.

Competitive analysis

We compared Olio, Buy Nothing, Nextdoor, and Too Good To Go, then used a SWOT analysis to choose a model. Business-to-consumer (B2C) would need community infrastructure and likely payments; consumer-to-consumer (C2C) was more flexible with lighter quality control. The team chose C2C.

Survey

A 14-day survey of 20 to 30 questions covered needs, usage, awareness, values, comfort with donated goods, and demographics.

174started the survey
138completed it
~45%had used a food-sharing platform

People aged 18–50 had the most platform experience, which fell with age, but past use didn't predict whether someone shared food. We focused the app on ages 18–50.

Interviews

I wrote the discussion guide and led seven semi-structured interviews over two weeks with a mix of experienced and first-time users. I coded the transcripts inductively in Userbit; a second researcher coded them independently to reduce bias, and we merged codes and affinity mapped the results.

What we learned

Sustainability and community drove use. Coordination was the biggest pain point, easy pickup mattered most to receivers, and people felt a stigma around free or discounted food.

These findings echoed the survey, where concerns about the safety or quality of donated food (27 respondents) and distance from pickup (26) were the top barriers to using food-sharing platforms.

Personas and IA

Research, Design, and Strategy built Giver and Taker personas from the survey, interviews, and Strategy's Jobs-to-be-Done analysis, which also sharpened our problem statement. In the final sprint, a remote closed card sort in Optimal Workshop (29 cards from the wireframes) helped us build a user-centered information architecture.

Impact

  • Answered the open questions: B2C or C2C, who the users are, and whether to share food, goods, or both.
  • Advocated for users: research led to stronger safety features and put community first.
  • Shaped design and strategy: findings set features, voice, and messaging instead of assumptions.

The team presented the final demo to the client, stakeholders, and the Tech Fleet community.

Case study · Restaurant reviews

DAX Dashboard

A review dashboard that helps diners choose faster. I scraped and analyzed tens of thousands of reviews, validated the findings with a survey, and turned them into the tiered metrics the dashboard is built on.

Role
UX Researcher
Timeline
May – Aug 2023 (Phase I)
How I joined
Applied directly to DAX's founder when they were looking for researchers
Deliverables
Competitive analysis, data scrape, data visuals, lo- and mid-fi wireframes; hi-fi MVP prototype by the design team
Tools
Octoparse, Excel, Voyant Tools, Figma
Methods
Competitive analysis · Web scraping · Text analysis · Survey (156 responses) · Metrics modeling · Wireframing

Context

A 2021 PowerReviews survey found that 99.9% of consumers read reviews at least sometimes before buying. The client wanted a restaurant platform that helps diners decide quickly and puts each restaurant's strongest stats up front. Our goals were to find diners' pain points, identify the metrics that define a good experience, and design a dashboard around them.

Competitive analysis

Each researcher covered different platforms; mine were Yelp, TripAdvisor, Foursquare, and OpenTable. None offered sophisticated filtering, and none visualized review data; a diner looking for great drink specials, for example, had no quick way to find them.

Data scrape

I scraped reviews of four Houston restaurants with Octoparse and analyzed them in Voyant Tools.

50,000+data points scraped
10recurring review themes

Reviews most often mentioned service, food, photos, ambiance, price, value, plate size, specials, location, and music. Three of these drive the overall rating:

Tier I → Tier II

Average rating (Tier I) rests on Food, Service, and Value (Tier II).

Survey and metrics

To validate the metrics, we surveyed diners about their dining experience, since that's what reviews describe. I designed the survey graphic and led distribution through restaurant, foodie, and travel groups on Facebook, Reddit, and Discord.

156responses in 10 days
84%completion rate

We turned the results into a pyramid. Its base became Tier III metrics, the details behind each Tier II metric, which diners can toggle on the dashboard to match their needs. Ambiance was tested too, but diners ranked it below the other three, so it didn’t become a core metric.

Designing the dashboard

I built several wireframe iterations from the survey and pyramid, then handed them and my notes to the design team. The final design shows Tier II metrics center-left with Tier III below, and lets diners filter reviews by any tier.

Reflections

Interviews would have added depth on how diners decide, and with more time I would have run eye-tracking tests to validate the layout.

Case study · Nonprofit website

Global Justice Ecology Project

GJEP is a nonprofit that exposes the root causes of social injustice, ecological harm, and economic domination of the Global South. We redesigned its website to explain its value clearly and simplified its navigation by combining related topics.

Role
UX Writer
Timeline
Apr – Aug 2024 (Phase II)
How I joined
Client project through Tech Fleet
Team
UX Writing team, working with UX Research and Design
Tools
Figma
Methods
Content audit · Client workshops · Message testing (2 rounds) · Content design · Copywriting

Content audit

Our audit of the existing site showed where to focus, where to collaborate, and what the research team should test. It also surfaced the problem that ran through the whole phase: the client struggled to communicate its mission and value.

Mission and value

To give every team a shared voice, we ran workshops to draft several mission statements and value propositions, then tested them with users.

After 2 rounds of testing

Users distrusted buzzy language like "global alliances" and "root causes" and wanted action words and evidence of results. We rewrote both statements to be specific, action-oriented, and grounded in GJEP's long track record.

The client valued that we named a problem they couldn't articulate and gave them a way to solve it.

Content design

To simplify the site's navigation, we combined related topics into single pages, such as merging Genetically Engineered Trees with Forest Protection. Each writer owned a page. Mine, Genetically Engineered Trees, had no existing copy, so I researched the issue from authoritative sources and borrowed language from other pages to keep the tone consistent. With the designers, I structured it as the issue, key accomplishments, and future work, with issue-specific calls to action at the top and bottom. A shared Figma file kept tone consistent across writers and let me work through layouts with designers in real time.

Lessons learned

  • Give stakeholders options, not blank pages. User-tested versions gave the client clear choices.
  • Keep work moving when input stalls. When meetings fell through, focused questions and outside sources filled every gap except the accomplishments timeline.
  • Use blocked time well. While parts of the work waited, I picked up other open problems.
